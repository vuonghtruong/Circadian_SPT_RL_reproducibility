Fig 1. Palatable-fluid preference and circadian rhythmicity across sex and age. (A) Schematic of the two-bottle preference assay. (B) Preference across fluid types. Bars show means ± SEM; dots represent individual animal means. In the water-only condition, preference denotes allocation to bottle A. The dashed line indicates equal allocation (0.5). Fluid preference was analyzed using a linear mixed model with fluid type, sex and their interaction as fixed effects and animal identity as a random intercept; coefficients and p-values are reported in the Results. Asterisks above bars indicate additional post-hoc comparisons with water, pooled across sexes, using animal-cluster-robust contrasts for sucrose and saccharin and Welch’s test for glucose, with BH FDR correction across three comparisons. (C) Group-level rhythmicity, expressed as p-values from 24-h OLS cosinor fits to group-mean preference profiles. (D) Emergence index: observed acrophase concentration divided by the mean concentration obtained after temporal permutation. (E–H) Female and (I–L) male preference profiles and acrophase distributions across age groups. Left panels show individual session trajectories (thin gray lines), group means ± SEM (blue), and fitted group-mean cosinor curves (orange). Right panels show individual acrophases on the circumference; filled symbols indicate significant individual cosinor fits (uncorrected p < 0.05), and open symbols indicate nonsignificant fits. Orange arrows represent mean resultant vectors. Blue and gray clouds represent bootstrapped and temporally permuted mean vectors, respectively; radial distance indicates phase concentration. Displays contain 500 bootstrap and 1,000 permutation draws; inference uses 2,000 and 1,999 draws, respectively. Counts indicate recording sessions, including repeated measurements of some animals. Gray shading marks reference hours 12–24, corresponding to the preceding dark phase for recordings in constant darkness. *q < 0.05, **q < 0.01, ***q < 0.001 for the BH-adjusted comparisons in B; individual fit symbols use nominal p as specified above; ns, not significant.

Fig 3. Simulated preference dynamics and empirical comparison of reinforcement-learning models with circadian modulation. (A) Mean daytime and nighttime preference ± SEM across 50 simulated runs per model. Simulations used 1,000 drinking opportunities, distributed as 200 during daytime and 800 during nighttime. Brackets indicate paired comparisons with BH FDR correction across five models; these tests describe simulation replicates rather than animal-level evidence. (B) Simulated preference aggregated into 3-h intervals, shown as means ± SEM across 500 runs. (C–G) Representative simulated choice trajectories for Random (C), Bias (D), Simple RL (E), circadian Reward RL (F), and Full RL (G), displayed using a 31-event moving average. Simple RL contains no explicit circadian term; Reward RL incorporates circadian reward modulation; Full RL additionally incorporates circadian modulation of choice sensitivity (inverse temperature, β). Lower β produces more stochastic choice or random exploration, whereas higher β strengthens selection of the higher-valued option. Learning states continue across chronological intervals. Gray shading indicates reference hours 12–24. Simulated events represent assumed drinking opportunities, not measured licks or trials. (H–J) Model comparison in 140 inferred animals, retaining one earliest eligible palatable-fluid recording per animal and excluding AD and restricted-feeding recordings. This cohort comprises 113 reference-condition animals and 27 additional phase-advance animals. Eight principal candidates were compared: Random, Bias, and switching/no-switch variants of Simple, Reward, and Full RL. (H) Mean Δpseudo-AIC ± SEM, ordered from lowest to highest mean score difference. The complexity-penalized score is −2ℓ + 2k, where ℓ is the intake-weighted pseudo-log-likelihood scaled to an assumed total of 1,000 events, and k is the number of fitted parameters. Δpseudo-AIC is calculated relative to each animal’s lowest-scoring model; lower values indicate better fits. (I) Number of animals for which each candidate achieved the lowest score. (J) Individual Δscores, with animals grouped by their best-fitting model. Colors use a square-root scale and are capped at Δscore = 100 for display. The horizontal axis gives animal order; model identity is shown on the vertical axis. NS denotes the no-switch variant. These comparisons quantify fit to the observed recordings; prediction of omitted measurements is evaluated separately in Fig 4. *q < 0.05, **q < 0.01, ***q < 0.001; ns, not significant.

Orange circles, magenta squares and teal triangles identify Control-genotype, AB and VB animals, respectively. Lines and large symbols show mean resultant vectors: angles indicate circular mean acrophases, and lengths indicate phase concentration. Colored arcs compare each genotype with Control using pooled mean-vector label-permutation tests (999,999 permutations). S2 Fig. Bottle controls and preference rhythmicity. (A) Initial bottle weights in the archived control sample, shown as mean ± SEM and individual measurements (10 measurements per bottle). The independent two-sample test gives t = 1.835, p = 0.0831; ns denotes p ≥ 0.05. These hand-entered archived bottle-weight arrays are separate from the reconciled animal cohort. (B) Total water-only and palatable-fluid intake in age-matched comparisons, shown as mean ± descriptive SEM with individual matching entries connected. Connections indicate age matching, not necessarily the same animal measured twice. Gray denotes males and pink females throughout B–D. There are 11 female and 9 male matched pairs, comprising 8 and 9 independent connected-animal components. Matching does not establish that water and palatable sessions belong to the same animal; animals measured in separate sessions can contribute to more than one matched pair. Connected-component-robust tests give p = 0.167 for females and p = 0.727 for males (BH-adjusted q across the two sex-specific tests = 0.334 and 0.727). (C) Bars show the permutation p value for group preference-phase organization in each sex and age group; open diamonds show the descriptive overlap of bootstrap and permutation phase concentrations, Pr(Rbootstrap ≤ Rpermutation). This statistic is not a p value. The dashed line marks 0.05. (D) Bars show the percentage of sessions with nominal individual preference cosinor p < 0.05; black horizontal marks show the percentage with BH q < 0.05. The dashed line marks 5%. Session counts for young, early middle-aged, middle-aged and old animals are 28, 23, 13 and 4 for females, and 35, 31, 16 and 2 for males. Nominal significant-session counts are 3, 1, 0 and 0 for females and 2, 3, 0 and 0 for males; no individual session meets BH q < 0.05 in these groups. Repeated sessions are retained in this descriptive age-cohort display.

S3 Fig. Summary of model development and generative framework. (A) Shared illustrative structure: observed total intake determines the temporal allocation of assumed events, and each model allocates these events between sucrose and water to generate preference. The profiles are schematic rather than recorded choices. (B) Bottle-position assignments alternate every 3 h within a session in switch variants; no-switch variants retain a fixed assignment. (C) The five principal model classes differ in learning and circadian modulation. Noise and swap-sensitivity labels are qualitative descriptions, not fitted quantities or empirical estimates. Full RL modulates reward and choice sensitivity with a shared phase and opposite signs. Linear and day/night Bias controls, parameter counts, and the implemented expected-update recurrence are specified in Methods. The assumed events do not constitute observed independent drinking choices.
